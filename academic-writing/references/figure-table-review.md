# Figures, tables, and manuscript layout

A visual should help readers perceive a comparison or mechanism used in the argument. Scope the work to the request: editing a sentence does not require redesigning every figure.

## Choose the representation

Use a table for exact results and lookup, a plot for trends and trade-offs, a diagram for information flow or responsibility, and a case for a concrete mechanism or failure. Do not turn a short inventory into a decorative diagram. Give each artifact a clear principal message.

The corpus often uses an early figure to preview the claim and another to explain the mechanism. Planner Matters highlights allocation and performance; StructAgent shows progress through shared state; CD-LAM pairs performance dimensions with the conditioning problem; RSIAgent shows exploration and memory reuse. These are useful choices, not a required figure or panel count.

## Match the visual to the comparison

Keep axes, units, baseline identities, budgets, and aggregation visible. Use the same scale when direct comparison is intended. Distinguish published references from matched runs. Define a diagnostic measuring a different object than the task score instead of making its columns appear interchangeable.

Arrange table rows by the comparison readers should make. Use stable precision, clear model blocks, and unambiguous best-value markings. Preserve results during prose polishing. In diagrams, label operations with verbs, distinguish inputs from state, and give arrows a consistent meaning.

## Give captions and paragraphs different jobs

The caption identifies the measurement or mechanism, scope, and essential encodings. Include conditions needed to interpret it on its own. Nearby prose explains the pattern and implication. Neither should recite every value.

Keep sample sizes and stages where they matter. A monitoring-subset curve must not masquerade as a full-test result. A caption can state a concise finding when actually shown. Avoid repeating every implementation exception in every caption.

## Inspect the rendered page

Read labels at final paper size. Repair collisions, cropping, mismatched units, excessive caption density, and visuals drifting into the next subsection. Preserve the existing style unless redesign is requested. Edit graphical sources and keep generated assets synchronized with their data.

In the author's main text, avoid one- or two-word paragraph endings. Judge wrapped text against its current column width. Prefer a final line over half that width and normally at least one third. Apply this to prose captions when practical. Exclude equations, table cells, diagram labels, verbatim examples, and the bibliography from automatic prose-tail judgments.

Fix endings with meaningful rewriting. Do not add padding, hard line breaks, global tracking changes, or smaller fonts. If shortening prose moves a wrapped figure or table, check that it stays beside the right discussion and inside the page. Use modest local placement or space reservation when needed, and inspect the whitespace it creates. Respect the user's appendix scope.

## Verify what changed

Compile and check undefined references and overflow. View affected and adjacent pages changed by floats. Confirm caption meaning, numeric data, equations, and cited panels remain consistent. A clean log does not establish readable layout. A local cosmetic change does not require scientific experiments or a full independent audit.
